# necessity — Does every part earn its place for the reader's decision?
> Cites: res_technical-writing.md

## Fires on
General lens (no tag) — select for any prose or content artifact that could carry filler. The cutting test; pairs with depth-sufficiency (its inverse — that flags what's missing, this cuts what's surplus). Together they aim at "as much as the decision needs, no more."

## Attacks
- Throat-clearing / filler: "it should be noted that", "in order to", preambles that say nothing (see res_technical-writing's blocklist).
- Generic background the stated reader already has, or that doesn't serve the decision.
- Restated obvious: content that repeats what's already clear from context.
- Present-because-easy: sections included because they were cheap to generate, not because the reader needs them.
- Redundant restatement: the same point made two or three times in different words.

## Measure
Cuttable words ÷ total words, where cuttable means the reader's decision is unchanged without them.
Report as a percentage. Above 20%, or filler where a length limit exists = should-fix. Above 40%,
with the point buried under it = blocker.

## Evidence of attack (clean-pass proof)
Apply the cutting test: for the candidate filler, confirm that removing it would lose information the reader needs to act — if not, it goes. Name what you cut (or flag) with the passage, or confirm each section earns its place. Not "concise enough."

## Severity guide
- blocker: rare — only if filler is so pervasive the actual content is buried and the reader can't find the point.
- should-fix: repeated filler, generic background, or restated-obvious sections that pad the artifact and waste the reader's time.
- nit: a stray throat-clearing phrase or one redundant sentence.
